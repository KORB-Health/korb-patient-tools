Patient Consent Form for Peptide Therapy

Peptide therapy involves the use of specific peptides—short chains of amino acids—to influence various physiological processes in the body. Peptides may be administered to support goals such as improving immune function, enhancing muscle growth, promoting tissue repair, optimizing metabolism, or addressing symptoms of aging or chronic conditions. Peptides are typically administered via subcutaneous injections, oral formulations, or other methods as prescribed.

The goals of peptide therapy may include:

  • Enhancing physical performance, muscle mass, or recovery.
  • Supporting metabolic health, weight management, or energy levels.
  • Improving skin health, immune function, or cognitive performance.
  • Addressing specific medical conditions (e.g., chronic inflammation, hormonal imbalances).
  • Other individualized goals discussed with your provider.

Peptide therapy, like any medical treatment, carries potential risks and side effects. These vary depending on the specific peptide(s) used, dosage, duration of treatment, and your personal health history. Possible risks and side effects include but are not limited to:

  • Local Reactions: Pain, redness, swelling, or irritation at the injection site.
  • Systemic Reactions: Headaches, nausea, fatigue, or dizziness.
  • Hormonal Imbalances: Potential overstimulation of hormone pathways (e.g., growth hormone), which may lead to unintended effects.
  • Allergic Reactions: Rare risk of allergic responses to peptides or their components.
  • Long-Term Risks: Limited long-term data exists for some peptides, and unknown risks may emerge with prolonged use.
  • Other Side Effects: Changes in appetite, sleep patterns, or mood.

Peptide therapy may provide benefits, such as:

  • Improved physical function, energy, or recovery time.
  • Enhanced immune or metabolic health.
  • Support for specific medical conditions or wellness goals.
  • Potential improvement in quality of life.

The effectiveness of peptide therapy varies between individuals, and no specific outcomes are guaranteed.

Alternatives to peptide therapy may include:

  • Lifestyle modifications (e.g., diet, exercise, sleep optimization).
  • Conventional medications or therapies (e.g., hormone replacement, anti-inflammatory drugs).
  • Nutritional supplements or other integrative approaches.
  • Monitoring symptoms without treatment.
  • Other options discussed with your provider

Your provider will work with you to develop a personalized peptide therapy plan, which may include:

  • Type of peptide(s)
  • Dosage and administration (e.g., injections, oral, nasal spray)
  • Monitoring schedule (e.g., blood tests, follow-up visits)

You will be monitored regularly to assess the safety and effectiveness of treatment. Adjustments to your plan may be made based on your response and any side effects.

As a patient, you agree to:

  • Administer peptides as prescribed and report any side effects promptly.
  • Attend follow-up appointments and complete recommended tests or monitoring.
  • Inform your provider of any changes in your health, medications, or symptoms.
  • Discuss any plans to stop or change treatment with your provider.

Your provider agrees to:

  • Monitor your health and adjust treatment as needed.
  • Provide education about peptide therapy and answer your questions.
  • Respect your autonomy and preferences in decision-making.

Some peptides may be used off-label, meaning they are prescribed for purposes not specifically approved by regulatory agencies like the FDA. Additionally, certain peptides may be classified as investigational or have limited clinical data supporting their use. Your provider has discussed the regulatory status of the peptide(s) in your treatment plan and any implications for safety or efficacy.

You are being offered healthcare services using “telehealth” technology. This form explains your rights, the risks and benefits of telehealth, and your options. Please read it carefully before signing. If you have any questions, please feel free to call our office at 888-959-7299.

1. Nature of Telehealth

Telehealth involves the use of electronic communications (audio, video, or other digital means) to deliver medical or mental health care when you and the provider are not in the same physical location.

Services may include consultation, diagnosis, treatment, follow-up, and patient education.

2. Provider Disclosure

You will be informed of the name, credentials, and location of your provider before or at the start of the session.

All persons present during the session, including trainees or assistants, will be identified and you will have the right to request their exclusion.

3. Patient Rights

You may request an in-person appointment instead of telehealth, when reasonably available.

You may withdraw consent or stop telehealth care at any time but prior to future visits, a new consent to treat will have to be authorized.

You have the right to access your medical records resulting from a telehealth encounter, as provided by law.

4. Risks and Limitations

Technology may fail or be interrupted (e.g., poor connection, hardware/software malfunction).

Security protocols may fail, potentially exposing personal information.

Telehealth may not be as effective as in-person visits for certain conditions.

If a telehealth visit is deemed clinically inappropriate, you may be asked to schedule an in-person appointment.

5. Privacy and Confidentiality

All communications will comply with HIPAA and applicable privacy laws.

Security measures will be in place to protect your information.

You are responsible for ensuring privacy on your end (e.g., secure location, using private internet connection).

6. Billing and Insurance

Telehealth services shall be paid out-of-pocket.

7. Emergency Situations

Telehealth is not appropriate for emergency medical conditions. In case of an emergency, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.

      What lands in the chart

      The intake is delivered into Tebra as a PDF in the patient's documents. This is the provider summary page of that PDF: the ten KORB note sections, in order, each as its own labelled block so a provider can select one and copy it into the matching Tebra field. On this screen the Copy buttons do it for you. In the real PDF the provider selects the block and copies it, which works in any PDF reader as long as the file is generated as text.

      What changes

      Same clinical questions either way. The difference is when they get answered and what the provider opens.

      Today

      1. Patient books from the website, often with no intake
      2. Provider opens a blank chart
      3. A flat PDF prints every answer option with an X by the chosen one
      4. Provider reads it question by question
      5. Note rebuilt by hand through Text Blaze

      Nothing is copyable · the visit pays for the typing

      With intake screening

      1. Non-candidates learn before they hold a slot
      2. Demographics and history arrive first
      3. PDF prints only the answers, in note-section blocks
      4. Provider copies a block into each Tebra field
      5. Provider writes the assessment and the plan
